# What Is Doomscrolling?

**Doomscrolling is the compulsive habit of continuously scrolling through negative, distressing, or anxiety-inducing content on social media and news sites — even when it makes you feel worse.** It's the experience of lying in bed at midnight, reading about disasters, political crises, and worst-case scenarios, knowing you should stop, and being unable to look away. The term combines "doom" (a sense of impending catastrophe) with "scrolling" (the act of endlessly moving through a digital feed).

## Where the Term Came From

"Doomscrolling" entered mainstream vocabulary in early 2020, though the behavior is older than the word. As COVID-19 spread globally and people were locked in their homes with nothing but their phones, the combination of genuine uncertainty and infinite news feeds created a perfect storm of compulsive scrolling.

Financial analyst and Twitter user @Depressing_Feed is often credited with popularizing the term, though scattered uses appeared on social media as early as 2018. By mid-2020, "doomscrolling" was everywhere — covered by major outlets, discussed by mental health professionals, and added to dictionaries. The Oxford English Dictionary considered it for its 2020 Words of the Year list.

The word stuck because it named something millions of people were doing but didn't have a word for. Once you could name it, you could recognize it — and recognizing it is the first step to changing it.

## Why Doomscrolling Is So Addictive

Doomscrolling isn't a character flaw. It's the result of several psychological mechanisms working together to keep you trapped in the feed.

### Negativity Bias

Your brain is wired to pay more attention to threats than to positive information. This is an evolutionary adaptation — for your ancestors, missing a predator was fatal while missing a nice sunset was fine. Negative news triggers this threat-detection system, and your brain responds by wanting more information: "What else is dangerous? What do I need to know?"

The problem is that an infinite feed always has more negative information. Your threat-detection system never gets the "all clear" signal, so you keep scrolling, looking for resolution that never comes.

### Variable Reward Schedules

Like all infinite scroll feeds, doomscrolling exploits variable reward schedules. Most posts are mildly concerning but unremarkable. But occasionally one is genuinely alarming, insightful, or rage-inducing. Your brain can't predict which post will be "the one," so it keeps checking — the same mechanism that makes slot machines addictive.

### The Illusion of Preparedness

Doomscrolling often feels like research. "If I just read enough about this situation, I'll understand it. I'll be prepared." But the feed is designed to keep you scrolling, not to help you reach understanding. Each article leads to three more. Each alarming development raises more questions. You never reach the point where you feel informed enough to stop.

This illusion of preparedness is particularly insidious because it reframes compulsive behavior as responsible behavior. You're not wasting time — you're staying informed. Except you're not becoming more informed. You're becoming more anxious.

### Loss of Agency

When the world feels out of control, scrolling through information about it provides a false sense of doing something. You can't solve a pandemic or a political crisis from your phone, but reading about it feels more productive than doing nothing. In reality, the time spent doomscrolling is time not spent on things you can actually control — your sleep, your relationships, your mental health.

## The Health Effects of Doomscrolling

### Anxiety and Stress

Research is clear on this point. A 2020 study published in Health Communication found that people who consumed more than 2 hours of COVID-related news daily reported significantly higher anxiety, depression, and stress scores than those who limited their consumption. The relationship was dose-dependent — more doomscrolling meant more anxiety.

### Sleep Disruption

Doomscrolling often happens at night, when you're already tired and your impulse control is lowest. The combination of blue light exposure, emotional activation from negative content, and mental stimulation makes it one of the worst possible activities before sleep. Your brain needs to wind down before bed; doomscrolling does the opposite.

### Learned Helplessness

Repeated exposure to problems you can't solve trains your brain toward helplessness. Psychologist Martin Seligman's research showed that organisms exposed to uncontrollable negative events eventually stop trying to change their situation — even when change becomes possible. Constant exposure to overwhelming, uncontrollable bad news can produce the same effect.

### Physical Symptoms

Chronic stress from doomscrolling manifests physically: elevated cortisol, tension headaches, jaw clenching, shallow breathing, increased heart rate, and weakened immune function. These aren't abstract — they're measurable physiological responses to sustained negative input.

## How to Stop Doomscrolling

### 1. Set Information Boundaries

Choose 2-3 specific times per day to check news (morning, lunch, evening). Set a timer for 10-15 minutes per session. When the timer goes off, stop — even if you haven't "finished." You will never finish. The feed is infinite by design.

### 2. Use a Mindful Intervention

Install [Intently](/download) and flag your news and social media apps. The brief pause before opening creates a moment where you can ask: "Am I checking this intentionally, or am I falling into the scroll?" That awareness alone often breaks the cycle.

### 3. Curate Ruthlessly

Unfollow accounts that consistently post alarming, sensationalized, or outrage-driven content. Follow sources that provide context and analysis rather than raw emotional reaction. Your feed is a diet for your mind — curate it like you'd curate what you eat.

### 4. Replace the Ritual

Doomscrolling often fills a specific slot — the last 30 minutes before bed, the first thing in the morning, the dead time between tasks. Identify when you doomscroll and replace that specific slot with a specific alternative: a book, a podcast, a walk, a conversation. The more concrete the replacement, the more likely it is to stick.

### 5. Turn Off News Notifications

Breaking news notifications create a Pavlovian response: buzz → check → anxiety → scroll. Turn all of them off. The news will still be there when you choose to check it at your scheduled time. Nothing is so urgent that it can't wait 2-3 hours.

### 6. Recognize the Feeling

Learn to notice the emotional signature of doomscrolling: a tightening in your chest, growing anxiety, a sense of urgency mixed with helplessness, the feeling that you need to keep reading but can't explain why. When you notice that feeling, that's your cue to stop. Put the phone down, take five deep breaths, and do something in the physical world.

## Doomscrolling vs. Staying Informed

There's a legitimate difference between being informed and being consumed. Informed looks like: reading a trusted source once or twice a day, understanding the key developments, and moving on with your life. Consumed looks like: checking every 20 minutes, reading the same story from six different angles, scrolling through reactions and predictions, and feeling worse each time.

The question isn't whether to follow the news. It's whether the way you're following it is adding to your understanding or just adding to your anxiety. If you can't articulate what you learned in the last 30 minutes of scrolling, you were doomscrolling.

## The Bottom Line

Doomscrolling feels productive, but it's the opposite of productive. It increases anxiety without increasing understanding, disrupts sleep without providing rest, and creates the illusion of preparedness while actually fostering helplessness.

The solution isn't ignorance — it's intentionality. Set boundaries around your information consumption, recognize when scrolling stops being useful, and give yourself permission to disengage from the feed without feeling irresponsible.
