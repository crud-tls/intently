You sit down to read an article and three paragraphs in, your hand drifts toward your phone. You start a work task and before you've gotten anywhere, you've checked Slack, glanced at Instagram, and forgotten what you were doing. A book that would have taken you a weekend now takes you a month because you can't get through more than a few pages without your mind — or your hand — wandering.

If this sounds familiar, you're not imagining it. Something has changed about how your brain handles attention. The question is whether it's permanent, and what you can do about it.

## Is attention really shrinking?

### What does the research say?

Let's start with what we actually know, because this topic is surrounded by myths.

The most-cited claim -- "human attention spans are now shorter than a goldfish's" -- traces back to a 2015 Microsoft Canada report that quoted a source with no scientific basis. The "8 seconds" figure is widely regarded as a myth, and the report's other numbers should be treated with caution too. It's a zombie statistic that won't die.

What has better support in research:

- **Gloria Mark at UC Irvine** has tracked how long people stay on a single screen before switching. Her studies reported that the average fell from a couple of minutes in the mid-2000s to under a minute in more recent years.
- **A 2019 study in Nature Communications** (Lorenz-Spreen and colleagues) found that public attention to trending topics, across several platforms, turns over faster than it used to.

So: the goldfish thing is nonsense, but there is reasonable evidence that we switch more often. Whether that has permanently changed our ability to focus is much less clear.

### What is actually happening?

The issue isn't that your brain is "broken." It's that it's been retrained.

Your brain is an adaptation machine. Your brain adapts to what you repeatedly do -- this is neuroplasticity, in plain terms. If you spend years rapidly switching between apps, notifications and content streams, you get practised at switching and out of practice at sustaining. Researchers disagree on how much lasting change this causes, so treat the following as a helpful picture rather than settled fact.

Think of it like a runner who stops running and starts only doing sprints. After a while, they can sprint fine, but they've lost their endurance. Your brain hasn't lost the ability to focus; it's lost the practice.

## What does constant switching cost?

### Task-Switching Isn't Multitasking

What most people call "multitasking" is actually rapid task-switching — your brain can't truly process two cognitive tasks simultaneously. Each switch between tasks carries a cost:

- **Refocus time**: Gloria Mark's research is often quoted as finding that it can take around 23 minutes to fully return to a task after an interruption. Exact numbers vary, but even a 30-second phone check can derail deep work for far longer than 30 seconds.
- **More mistakes**: Switching tends to cost accuracy as well as time, especially on complex tasks.
- **More tiredness**: Many people find that heavy switching leaves them more mentally drained by mid-afternoon.

### Stanford's Multitasking Research

Clifford Nass and colleagues at Stanford studied heavy media multitaskers in 2009 and found something unexpected: they did worse on some tests of filtering out irrelevant information and switching between tasks. Later studies have been more mixed, and the research is correlational, so it does not prove that multitasking causes the difference.

Still, many people feel the pattern: constant switching creates an impression of productivity while the work itself suffers.

### The Shallow Work Trap

Cal Newport's concept of "deep work" — sustained, distraction-free focus on cognitively demanding tasks — is becoming rare precisely because shallow work (quick emails, Slack messages, brief phone checks) feels productive while consuming the same hours.

Many people spend their days in a state of "continuous partial attention" -- never fully focused on anything, never fully disengaged. It's tiring and often unproductive, but it feels busy, which is easy to confuse with productive. For more on this at work, see [digital wellbeing at work](/blog/digital-wellbeing-at-work/).

## How do phones affect focus?

### The "brain drain" idea

A 2017 study from the University of Texas (Ward and colleagues) reported that people performed slightly worse on attention tests when their phone was on the desk, compared with when it was in another room. The authors called this "brain drain". Later attempts to replicate it have had mixed results, so it is not settled, but the practical advice is cheap and sensible: if the phone is out of sight, you spend less effort not checking it.

### Notification Conditioning

Notifications train you to associate phone sounds and vibrations with possible rewards. Many people even report "phantom vibrations", the sensation of a buzz that never happened, which shows how tuned in we get.

### Why does slow work feel so boring?

A phone offers small, frequent rewards on demand. After a day of that, slower activities such as reading, conversation or focused work can feel understimulating by comparison. Whether this reflects changes in the brain's reward systems is not settled, but the experience is common, and it usually eases when you spend some time away from constant stimulation.

## How do I rebuild my focus?

Habits that built the distraction can be undone with practice. Think of it as endurance training for your attention.

### 1. Start With Reading

Reading a physical book is one of the most effective focus-rebuilding exercises because it's linear (no hyperlinks to chase), sustained (no natural stopping points every 15 seconds), and immersive (engages imagination in ways screens don't).

Start small — even 10 minutes a day. If you can't get through a page without your mind wandering, that's normal and expected. Gently redirect your attention back, like you would in meditation. Gradually extend the time. Many people find they can gradually work up to longer reading sessions over a few weeks.

### 2. Single-Task Deliberately

Pick one task. Do only that task. When the urge to check your phone or switch to something else arises — and it will — notice the urge without acting on it. Return to the task.

This is harder than it sounds because your brain has been trained to switch. The discomfort you feel is the pull of constant stimulation. It usually passes, and each time you sit with it and stay focused, you're practising sustained attention.

### 3. Progressive Focus Training

Use a modified Pomodoro technique:

- **Week 1**: 15 minutes of focused work, 5-minute break
- **Week 2**: 20 minutes focused, 5-minute break
- **Week 3**: 25 minutes focused, 5-minute break
- **Week 4 onwards**: extend gradually if it feels right, whether to 45 or 90 minutes

The key is consistency over ambition. Fifteen minutes of actual focus beats two hours of distracted half-work.

### 4. Create a Distraction-Free Environment

Your environment determines your behavior more than your intentions. For focus sessions:

- Phone in another room (not just face-down — out of the room entirely)
- Close all unnecessary browser tabs
- Turn off desktop notifications
- Use full-screen mode for whatever you're working on
- If possible, use a separate device or browser profile for focused work

Use a pause on your phone and in your Chrome browser. You're not blocked; you're given a moment to notice what you're doing. The idea is explained in [the science of friction](/blog/science-of-friction/).

Over time, these moments of awareness compound. You start catching the impulse before your hand even reaches your phone.

### 6. Practise meditation

Some studies suggest regular short meditation may help sustained attention, though results are modest and vary. You don't need an app or a special setup -- sit quietly, focus on your breathing, and when your mind wanders (it will), gently return to the breath.

What you're practising is exactly the skill you need: noticing when attention has drifted and bringing it back.

### 7. Protect Your Peak Hours

Identify the couple of hours when your focus is naturally strongest (for many people, mid-morning). Guard those hours as best you can: no meetings, no phone, no email. Do your most important cognitive work during this window and let everything else fill the remaining hours.

## What should I expect along the way?

There is no reliable timetable, but many people describe a pattern like this:

- **The first few days:** uncomfortable. The urge to check your phone during focused work is strong and frequent. This is normal.
- **The first couple of weeks:** the urge starts spacing out, and reading becomes slightly easier.
- **After several weeks:** longer focus sessions feel more natural, and deep work can feel rewarding rather than painful.

It isn't linear. You'll have good days and bad days. A hypothetical example: Ines, a graduate student, starts with 15-minute blocks, phone in her bag. Week one is fidgety. By week three she is managing 30 minutes, and by week six she reads a chapter without noticing the time. Yours may look different.

If you find that you cannot concentrate even without your phone, or it comes with low mood, worry or sleep problems, speak to a doctor. Attention difficulties have many causes, and this article is not a diagnosis. See also [phone addiction and ADHD](/blog/phone-addiction-adhd/).

## The good news

For most people, attention isn't permanently lost. You adapted to an environment of constant stimulation, and you can adapt to one of sustained focus.

The key is creating the conditions for that readaptation: reducing digital interruptions, practicing sustained attention daily, and being patient with yourself as your brain relearns a skill it hasn't used much lately.
