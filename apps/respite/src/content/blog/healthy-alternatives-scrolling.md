Let's be honest about something first: you scroll for a reason. Maybe you're bored, maybe you're anxious, maybe you want connection, or maybe you just need your brain to do something that isn't the thing you're supposed to be doing. There's nothing wrong with any of those needs. The problem isn't that you want stimulation or comfort — it's that your phone has become the default answer to every single one of those needs.

The trick to replacing scrolling isn't just having a list of "better" activities. It's matching the replacement to what you're actually craving. A book won't help when you're lonely. A walk won't help when you're understimulated. So this guide is organized by the underlying need, not by some arbitrary ranking.

## What can I do instead of scrolling? Quick picks

| If you want... | Try this first |
|---|---|
| Something to do with a spare minute | A physical book or a puzzle (#1, #3) |
| Connection | Text or call one specific person (#5, #6) |
| To relax | A phone-free walk or slow breathing (#9, #10) |
| Real stimulation | A hobby or a strategy game (#13, #14) |
| Information | One long article or a podcast episode (#17, #18) |

Read on for all twenty, grouped by the need behind the scroll.

## What can I do when I'm bored?

Boredom is one of the most common scrolling triggers. Your brain encounters a moment of nothing and reflexively reaches for the easiest source of stimulation. The goal isn't to eliminate boredom — boredom is actually valuable for creativity and mental rest. The goal is to have better options than infinite scroll.

### 1. Read Something Physical

A book, a magazine, even a newspaper. Physical reading engages your brain differently than screen reading — there's no hyperlink to chase, no notification to pull you away, no algorithm deciding what comes next. Keep a book wherever you normally scroll: by your bed, on the couch, in your bag.

### 2. Sketch or Doodle

You don't have to be an artist. Grab a pen and a piece of paper and draw whatever comes to mind. Doodling lets your mind wander in a useful way, a bit like daydreaming. It's meditative without requiring you to sit still and do nothing.

### 3. Puzzles and Brain Games

Crosswords, Sudoku, Wordle on paper, a Rubik's cube, jigsaw puzzles. These scratch the same "give my brain something to chew on" itch that scrolling does, but they give your attention one thing to hold instead of fragmenting it.

### 4. Learn Something Specific

Instead of passively consuming random content, pick one thing you've always wanted to understand — how engines work, basic guitar chords, a new language phrase per day — and spend your boredom moments on that. Duolingo in 5-minute bursts is more rewarding than 20 minutes of TikTok, and you actually retain something.

## What can I do when I want connection?

A lot of scrolling is driven by loneliness disguised as socializing. You're watching other people's lives instead of participating in your own social world, and passive feed-watching rarely fills that gap. These alternatives create real connection.

### 5. Text One Specific Person

Not a group chat, not a social media comment. Open your messages and reach out to one person you haven't talked to in a while. Ask them something real — how their new job is going, how their kid is doing, what they're reading. One genuine exchange is worth more than an hour of scrolling through acquaintances' highlight reels.

### 6. Call Someone

This feels weirdly radical in 2026, but picking up the phone and calling someone — even for five minutes — often creates a connection that texting can't match. Hearing someone's voice is warmer than a text. Start with the people who would be happy to hear from you.

### 7. Write a Letter or a Long Message

Not a text — something with actual thought in it. Tell someone why you appreciate them, catch up properly, share something meaningful. The act of writing to a specific person is inherently more connecting than broadcasting to everyone.

### 8. Go Where People Are

Coffee shop, park, library, community event. Even if you don't talk to anyone, being around other humans satisfies social needs that screens can't. If you're up for it, strike up a conversation. Most people are friendlier than we assume.

## What can I do when I need to relax?

Scrolling feels relaxing in the moment, but many people say it leaves them more drained, not less. True relaxation involves your body actually slowing down, which is hard when your brain is processing a new piece of content every few seconds.

### 9. Take a Walk — Without Your Phone

Even 10 minutes. A short walk is one of the simplest ways to shake off stress. Leave your phone at home or keep it in your pocket on silent. Let your mind wander. Notice things. This is the reset your brain is actually looking for when you reach for Instagram.

### 10. Breathing Exercises

Four counts in, hold for four, four counts out. Repeat five times. It takes about 90 seconds and slow breathing is widely used to help the body settle — the opposite of the keyed-up state scrolling can leave you in. Apps can guide you, but you don't need one. Just breathe slowly.

### 11. Listen to Music (Not as Background)

Put on headphones, pick an album or playlist you love, close your eyes, and just listen. Not as background to scrolling. Not while doing something else. Music as a primary activity is deeply relaxing and emotionally regulating in ways that passive content consumption isn't.

### 12. Stretch or Do Light Yoga

Five minutes of stretching reverses the physical tension that accumulates from phone posture (the hunched shoulders, the stiff neck, the compressed spine). YouTube has thousands of 5-minute routines, but honestly, just reaching for the ceiling and touching your toes a few times is a good start.

## What can I do when I want stimulation?

Sometimes your brain is genuinely understimulated and needs something interesting. Scrolling provides that — but in a junk-food way that leaves you wanting more without ever feeling satisfied. These alternatives provide real stimulation.

### 13. Pick Up a Hobby You've Been Meaning to Start

Guitar, cooking a new recipe, woodworking, knitting, photography, gardening. The barrier to starting most hobbies is lower than you think. You don't need to be good at it. The act of learning and creating something with your hands provides deep satisfaction that passive consumption never will.

### 14. Play a Strategy Game

Chess, a card game, a board game, even a video game that requires actual thinking (not just reaction time). Strategy games ask you to plan and decide, which is the opposite of passive scrolling. Many people feel sharper afterward, not drained.

### 15. Cook or Bake Something

Following a recipe engages multiple senses and requires enough attention to pull you out of your head. You end up with something tangible. Even making a slightly more elaborate version of a meal you'd normally throw together counts.

### 16. Write Something

Journal entry, short story, thoughts about something you've been processing, a review of something you watched. Writing organizes your thinking in ways that consuming content doesn't. You don't need to share it with anyone.

## What can I do when I want information?

The "I just want to check the news" scroll that turns into 45 minutes is one of the most common patterns. You have a legitimate desire for information, but the delivery mechanism hijacks it.

### 17. Read One Long-Form Article Instead of 50 Headlines

Pick one topic you care about and read a thorough piece on it — a magazine feature, a long blog post, an essay. You'll understand the topic far better than scrolling through dozens of headlines, and you'll actually remember what you read.

### 18. Listen to a Podcast Episode

Podcasts deliver information in a format that doesn't hijack your attention the same way visual feeds do. You can listen while walking, cooking, or doing chores. Pick a specific episode about something you're curious about rather than just hitting play on whatever's trending.

### 19. Watch a Documentary or Educational Video (Intentionally)

The key word is "intentionally." Choosing to watch a specific documentary is different from falling into a YouTube autoplay hole. Pick the thing, watch the thing, then stop. Having a clear endpoint prevents the drift into passive consumption.

### 20. Have a Real Conversation About Something You're Curious About

Ask a friend, family member, or colleague about something they know more about than you. People love sharing expertise, and you get information filtered through a relationship rather than an algorithm. You'll remember the conversation long after you'd have forgotten a social media post.

## How do I make the switch stick?

Knowing alternatives isn't enough — you need to make them easier to reach for than your phone. A few things that help:

**Redesign your environment.** Keep a book on your nightstand instead of a charger. Put a journal on the coffee table. Leave a guitar out of its case. The easier the alternative is to access, the more likely you are to choose it.

**Use friction on your phone.** Apps like [Respite](/download/) put a short pause before the apps you pick — a moment where you can choose one of these alternatives instead. It's free on iPhone, Android and Chrome. That gap between impulse and action is where the new habit gets built; see [the science of friction](/blog/science-of-friction/) for why.

**Don't try to replace all scrolling at once.** Pick one situation — morning scrolling, lunch break scrolling, bedtime scrolling — and introduce one alternative there. Once that feels natural, tackle the next one. For specific situations, see [stopping scrolling at night](/blog/stop-scrolling-at-night/) and [how to stop doomscrolling](/blog/how-to-stop-doomscrolling/).

**Expect the urge.** You'll feel the pull toward your phone. That's normal. The urge is the habit loop firing. It doesn't mean you need your phone — it means the habit is still active. Each time you choose an alternative, the urge gets a little weaker.

## What's the bigger picture?

None of these alternatives are magic. The point isn't to replace one compulsive behavior with another. It's to rebuild your menu of options so that when you feel bored, anxious, lonely, or understimulated, your brain has multiple paths to choose from — not just the one that leads to your phone.

Over time, the scroll-first reflex weakens. Not because you're forcing it, but because your brain has rediscovered that there are better ways to meet the needs it's been trying to meet through your phone all along.
